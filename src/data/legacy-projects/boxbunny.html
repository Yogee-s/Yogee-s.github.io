02

Building the punch-recognition model

The challenge: teach a small onboard computer to tell jabs, hooks, uppercuts and blocks apart in a split second, while gloves hide the hands.

Record punches, then label every one

I recorded boxers with a depth camera and built my own tool to label every punch.

An early test exposed the big problem straight away: boxing gloves hide the wrists, so normal skeleton tracking loses the hands.

Exploring input features

Every approach failed in a different way, and each failure pointed to the next idea.

First, eight ways to "see" a punch

The same clip, shown eight ways. Each panel is a different kind of input the model could learn from: skeletons, 3D blocks (voxels), cropped images, outlines and glove tracking.

How the nine iterations played out

Pose, pixels and voxels each failed on their own. Fusing voxels with pose fixed both weaknesses.

Pose (iter 1–2)
↓
1. 2D Pose + LSTM ✗ Wrong camera angle
↓
2. 3D Pose (depth lifting, ~67%) ✗ Glove occlusion blocks wrists
↓
Lesson: skeleton joints carry hand identity, but pose alone is not reliable under occlusion.
Pose returns later ↓
Raw Pixels (iter 3–4)
↓
3. Raw RGBD + 3D CNN (87.2%) ✗ 88M params, too large for Jetson
↓
4. RGBD + Segmentation ✗ Still pixel-based, still required Colab
↓
Lesson: need compact, engineered features; raw pixels are too heavy for edge deployment.
Dead end, redirected the search
Voxels (iter 5–7)
↓
5. Z-Mask (2D depth grid) ✗ Lost 3D spatial information
↓
6. Voxel + Colour (~96%) ✗ Impractical coloured gloves ★ Insight: voxels need hand info
↓
7. Voxel-Only (80.6%) ✗ Left/right confusion (34% jab error) ★ Insight: need pose back for L/R
↓
Lesson: 3D motion is the right primary signal, but voxels alone cannot distinguish left from right hand.
8. Voxel + Pose Fusion · 97.3% Pose solves left/right confusion. Voxels capture 3D motion. Full circle. Works with any gloves · YOLO finds the wrist visually.
↓
9. Production (v11) · 96.8% deployed TensorRT FP16, block-annotation fix, parallel threads.

03

Running it live on the robot

A new camera frame arrives every 33 ms, and the robot has to react before the next one.

Per-frame budget · 33 ms ceiling for 30 fps

BeforePyTorch
YOLO26n-pose ~33 ms ~8 ms
~41 ms · misses 30 fps
AfterTensorRT FP16
YOLO26n-pose ~16 ms ~8 ms 9 ms spare
~24 ms · fits 30 fps

YOLO26n-pose · finds the skeletonPunch model · Voxel-Pose TransformerThe punch model needs YOLO's output, so they run back to back. Converting YOLO to TensorRT is what made it fit.

Everything runs as 10 ROS 2 nodes: camera perception, punch detection from the impact sensors (IMU), camera + IMU fusion, motor control and the link to the phone dashboard.

04

Phone app + on-device AI coach

Feedback a boxer can actually use, without taking their gloves off.

App A phone dashboard, built end-to-end

  1. Scan Scan the QR code on the robot's screen.
  2. Open The dashboard opens in your phone browser. Nothing to install.
  3. Any network Gym Wi-Fi or mobile data. Still works locally with no internet.
  4. Control + review Start drills, see your stats and chat with the AI coach.

Coach An AI coach that lives on the robot

A small language model (Gemma 4 E2B) runs on the robot itself, so nothing goes to the cloud.

At the CDE AI Fair, people wanted to chat with the coach, but typing on the large touchscreen mounted on the robot was clumsy. So the conversation moved to the phone, where asking questions is far easier.

Tuning Making a small AI give good advice

Out of the box, a 2-billion-parameter model gives vague, made-up tips. Two fixes:

  • A tuning tool I built to test prompts and settings side by side against real training sessions
  • A boxing handbook it can look things up in (17 documents, via RAG), so it quotes real technique instead of making things up
